# RupeeRise — Product Design Case Study

## From financial literacy to more deliberate financial decisions

RupeeRise began as a financial-literacy product for Gen Z and Millennials.

The initial assumption was straightforward:

> **Young people make poor financial decisions because they don't know enough about money.**

That led me toward a product built around education, budgeting, savings, investment discovery, finance content and mentorship.

Later, while revisiting the project for my portfolio, I returned to the interview findings and conducted additional secondary research. That changed how I interpreted the problem.

People were already consuming financial content, including content from finfluencers, while making real investment decisions. Some participants also described FOMO, overtrading and difficulty judging risk.

The question shifted from:

> **How do we give people more financial knowledge?**

to:

> **How might we help people make more deliberate decisions when information, uncertainty and emotion collide?**

RupeeRise became a conceptual exploration of that problem.

---

## A note on the redesign

RupeeRise was originally developed as a hackathon project and taken through a functional MVP.

I later revisited the project while building my portfolio, re-examined the interview findings and conducted additional secondary research. This led me to rethink the original problem framing and explore a behavioral-finance direction as a conceptual redesign.

### The project therefore has two phases

**Hackathon**

Research → Original RupeeRise → Functional MVP → AI quiz generation → Presentation

**Portfolio revisit**

Re-examined research → Secondary research → Reframed problem → Conceptual product redesign

This distinction is important: the behavioral-finance experience shown later in this case study was not the product that was integrated into the hackathon MVP.

# 01 — The Starting Point

## The hackathon framed the problem around financial literacy

RupeeRise was created during the Udyog Pramoda 14-Day Job Ready Challenge.

The “Smart Finance for Gen Z & Millennials” brief focused on poor financial decisions and financial literacy across budgeting, investments, debt and savings. The challenge required primary and secondary research, hypotheses, feature prioritization, user journeys, wireframes, an MVP, AI integration and a working prototype.

That gave me a clear starting hypothesis:

> **If young people are making poor financial decisions, they need better financial knowledge and tools.**

I didn't have evidence yet that this was the complete problem.

But it was a reasonable hypothesis to test.

---

# 02 — V1: The First Product

## RupeeRise started as a broader personal-finance product

The first version tried to support several parts of the user's financial journey.

### Learn

Users could:

- Read financial education content
- Browse Fin Shots
- Take daily finance quizzes
- Learn basic investment concepts

### Manage

Users could:

- See their available balance
- Track monthly budget
- Create savings goals
- Set financial reminders

### Invest

Users could:

- Discover investments
- Explore trending stocks
- Learn about investment opportunities

### Get guidance

Users could:

- Browse mentors
- Explore finance specialties
- Compare ratings and hourly rates
- Access free classes

The product was essentially:

> **Learn → Manage → Invest → Get guidance**

This made sense for the original problem statement.

But it left an important question unanswered:

> **Does having more financial information actually lead to better financial decisions?**

---

# 03 — Research

## I wanted to understand what was happening beyond the product screens

I conducted approximately **10 exploratory 1:1 interviews** with Gen Z and Millennials in person at a café.

The participants weren't one uniform group.

Some were already investing.

Some understood investment concepts.

Some were actively investing or trading.

The conversations were informal and directional. There were no photos or recordings, so I treated them as exploratory qualitative research rather than statistically representative research.

Two patterns stood out.

## Pattern 01 — Financial information was already everywhere

**At least 6 participants** agreed with the broader observation that financial content from finfluencers can leave people with fragmented or incomplete financial knowledge, even while they continue to invest or trade.

These findings are not mutually exclusive.

Someone could recognize fragmented financial information and also describe problems with their own decision-making.

The important point was that people weren't necessarily saying:

> **“I don't know anything about investing.”**

They were already consuming information.

They were watching creators, reading financial content and picking up individual pieces of advice.

But individual pieces of information don't automatically create a complete understanding of what to do in a particular financial situation.

That made me question my original assumption.

## Pattern 02 — Some participants still struggled at the decision moment

**At least 4 participants** described difficulties such as:

- FOMO
- Overtrading
- Difficulty judging risk
- Hesitation or fear around investing

I don't claim that all four were active traders.

The interviews weren't recorded or structured in a way that allows reliable participant-level reconstruction of exactly which behaviors and investment activities occurred together.

What mattered was the behavioral signal:

> **Knowing something about investing and making a stable decision in the moment are two different problems.**

That distinction became more interesting than the original education-only framing.

---

# 04 — The Assumption I Revisited

## Financial education wasn't wrong. It was incomplete.

The research didn't tell me to remove financial education.

It made me look at the journey more carefully.

A beginner may genuinely need:

- Financial concepts
- Budgeting
- Savings goals
- Investment education

But once someone starts making investment decisions, the problem can change.

They may need:

- Context
- Risk awareness
- A way to rehearse decisions
- Reflection before acting
- Feedback on their own behavior

So the original assumption:

> **Lack of financial knowledge → poor financial decisions**

became a broader hypothesis:

> **Fragmented information + uncertainty + emotion → potentially unstable decisions**

And the opportunity became:

> **How might we help young investors turn fragmented financial information into more deliberate financial decisions?**

This was a portfolio-stage reframing, not a claim that the hackathon product was completely pivoted during the event.

---

# 05 — From One Product to a Layered System

## The research didn't replace the original product. It changed how the pieces fit together.

I didn't want to throw away education, budgeting or goals simply because some participants described behavioral problems.

Instead, I designed RupeeRise as a progression through different levels of financial decision-making.

## Foundation — Build financial knowledge

The research still supported a foundational education layer.

If users are dealing with fragmented information, removing education doesn't solve the problem.

So the foundation remains:

- Financial concepts
- Budgeting
- Savings goals
- Basic investment education

**Research signal → product response**

Fragmented information  
→  
A structured financial foundation

## Bridge — Prepare before real exposure

The next question was:

> **What happens when someone moves from learning about investing to making an actual decision?**

The original product moved relatively directly from information toward investment discovery.

I wanted an intermediate step.

So the bridge became:

- Market context
- Historical scenarios
- Simulation
- Risk awareness

The reasoning was simple:

> **If the gap exists between knowing and doing, give users somewhere to practise the “doing” before greater exposure.**

This became the Simulation Lab.

## Behavioral Layer — Make decision patterns visible

The behavioral findings suggested another opportunity for users who have enough investment activity for patterns to emerge.

Instead of giving these users another content feed, I explored a system that makes their own behavior visible.

That became:

- Stability Score
- Thesis Log
- Behavioral Insights
- Contextual mentorship

The complete progression became:

> **Learn → Understand → Simulate → Decide → Reflect**

This progression is the central product architecture of the redesign.

---

# 06 — The Product Thesis

The redesign is built around three principles.

### 01 — Measure behavior, not just outcomes

Financial returns don't necessarily tell us whether a decision was disciplined.

### 02 — Add friction at the decision moment

If impulsive behavior is part of the problem, removing every barrier may work against the product's purpose.

### 03 — Reward discipline, not activity

If the product rewards users simply for trading more often, it can reinforce the behavior it is supposed to help them control.

These principles shaped the core product system.

---

# 07 — Stability Score

## What if making money isn't the same as making a good decision?

The easiest version of an investment score would be based on performance.

I rejected that approach.

Imagine:

### Decision A

High concentration  
No clear exit  
Strong FOMO  
**+20% return**

### Decision B

Diversified  
Clear thesis  
Defined risk  
**−3% return**

A performance-based system says:

> **A is better than B.**

But Decision A could have been lucky.

Decision B could represent a much more disciplined process.

So I separated:

> **Financial outcome**

from:

> **Decision quality**

## The decision

The Stability Score evaluates behavioral signals such as:

- Risk awareness
- Decision consistency
- Diversification
- Reaction patterns
- Financial discipline

**Profit is deliberately excluded as the primary signal.**

### Why a single score?

I considered exposing every behavioral dimension independently.

That gives users more detail, but it also makes the system harder to scan.

I chose the score as an **entry point**, with the underlying dimensions explaining why it changed.

So the experience becomes:

**Stability Score**

↓

**What changed?**

↓

**Which behavior contributed?**

↓

**What can I improve?**

The score isn't meant to classify someone as a “good” or “bad” investor.

It is meant to make invisible patterns easier to notice.

### Trade-off

A single score is easier to understand at a glance, but less expressive than a full behavioral dashboard.

I accepted that trade-off because the score is the **entry point to reflection**, not the complete explanation.

---

# 08 — Simulation Lab

## From consuming information to rehearsing decisions

The research made me question whether another education feed would solve the problem.

If people are already consuming financial content, another article doesn't necessarily bridge the gap between:

**Knowing**

and

**Doing**

So I introduced simulation.

Users encounter historical market scenarios and decide what they would do before taking greater exposure.

The experience becomes:

> **Scenario → Decision → Outcome → Reflection**

### Why simulation?

It creates a controlled space where users can practise applying financial knowledge.

The goal isn't to make investing more entertaining.

It is to make the **decision process visible**.

### Trade-off

Simulation adds another step.

That makes the journey slower.

But if the product's purpose is deliberate decision-making, I would rather add meaningful friction than optimize purely for transactional speed.

---

# 09 — Thesis Log

## The friction is the intervention

I considered making the Thesis Log optional.

That would make the flow faster.

But optional reflection is also easy to skip precisely when it could be useful.

So for meaningful simulated decisions, the user must articulate:

- Why am I taking this position?
- Why now?
- What do I expect?
- What would make me exit?

The interaction changes from:

> **Impulse → Action**

to:

> **Impulse → Reflection → Action**

### Why written reflection instead of a checklist?

A checklist would be faster.

But it would also allow users to mechanically confirm that they had “considered risk” without actually articulating their reasoning.

A short thesis makes the reasoning explicit.

It also creates something the system can revisit later:

> **What did you believe before you acted?**

### Trade-off

Mandatory reflection slows the flow and adds friction.

I accepted that because the friction itself supports the product thesis.

> **The friction isn't a usability failure. It's the intervention.**

---

# 10 — Behavioral Insights

## Don't just show what happened. Show how the user behaved.

A conventional investment dashboard can tell users:

- Portfolio return
- Market movement
- Gains and losses

But that doesn't necessarily help someone recognize their own behavioral patterns.

Behavioral Insights connects decisions over time.

For example:

**Repeated buying after rapid price movements**

↓

**Possible FOMO pattern**

↓

**Reflection**

Rather than saying:

> “You made a bad decision.”

the product surfaces an observation:

> **“This pattern appears repeatedly in your recent decisions.”**

The goal becomes:

> **Market information → self-awareness**

rather than:

> **Market information → more activity**

---

# 11 — Contextual Mentorship

## From mentor discovery to intervention

Mentorship existed in the original product as a directory-style experience.

The redesign changes its role.

Instead of asking users to browse mentors whenever they want advice, mentorship can become relevant when the product identifies a behavioral need.

The conceptual flow becomes:

**Behavioral pattern**

↓

**Relevant intervention**

↓

**Mentorship**

This makes mentorship contextual rather than passive.

For example, a repeated decline in stability could lead to an opportunity for targeted guidance rather than simply showing the user another list of mentors.

This was a conceptual direction, not a validated behavior.

---

# 12 — The Decision Loop

The individual features work together.

### Before the decision

**Simulation Lab**

Creates context and rehearsal.

### At the decision

**Thesis Log**

Creates deliberate reflection.

### After the decision

**Behavioral Insights**

Creates feedback.

### Over time

The user encounters future decisions with greater awareness of their own patterns.

> **Scenario → Reflection → Decision → Behavioral feedback → Next decision**

That became the core interaction loop of the redesign.

# 13 — Five Decisions That Shaped the Product

## 01 — Remove profit from the Stability Score

**Situation**

Profit is the easiest investment metric to understand.

**Alternative**

Score users primarily on portfolio performance.

**Decision**

Measure behavioral stability instead.

**Why**

A profitable decision can still be impulsive or poorly reasoned.

**Trade-off**

The score becomes less immediately intuitive than “how much money did I make?”

I accepted that because the product is trying to improve decision quality rather than reward lucky outcomes.

## 02 — Make the Thesis Log mandatory

**Situation**

Users could otherwise move from simulation toward action with little reflection.

**Alternative**

Make the thesis optional to reduce friction.

**Decision**

Require reflection before meaningful simulated decisions.

**Why**

Optional reflection is easiest to skip at exactly the moment it could be most useful.

**Trade-off**

The flow becomes slower.

That friction is intentional.

## 03 — Reward discipline, not frequency

The hackathon brief encouraged thinking about gamification.

The obvious mechanics were:

- Streaks
- Leaderboards
- Activity rewards

I chose not to use frequency as the progression mechanism.

Instead, progression is associated with:

- Reflection
- Consistency
- Risk awareness
- Diversification
- Decision discipline

The principle became:

> **Progress should signal discipline, not stimulation.**

## 04 — Make the premium boundary contextual

I didn't want premium to simply mean:

> **“More finance features.”**

The free tier can answer:

> **How am I doing?**

Premium should answer:

> **Why am I behaving this way, and what should I do differently?**

So premium connects:

**Market context + portfolio + decision history + behavioral patterns**

The value is therefore **personalized interpretation**, not simply more information.

This business model remains a hypothesis.

## 05 — Inform, don't punish

A behavioral score can easily become a judgment mechanism.

I chose to frame changes as information rather than punishment.

Instead of:

> **Your score dropped.**

the system should explain:

> **What changed → Why it changed → What behavior contributed → What can I improve?**

The goal is awareness rather than shame.

---

# 14 — What I Deliberately Chose Not to Build

Some product mechanics could increase engagement while working against RupeeRise's purpose.

### Trending-stock feeds

Could create urgency and FOMO.

### Influencer marketplace

Could reinforce herd behavior without enough context.

### Trading streaks

Could reward frequency rather than decision quality.

### Profit leaderboards

Could confuse luck with skill.

### Price-movement push alerts

Could create unnecessary urgency.

### Automatic punitive restrictions

Could remove user control instead of helping users understand their behavior.

The principle behind these decisions was:

> **A financial product shouldn't optimize engagement at the expense of decision quality.**

# 15 — Business Model

## Free gives awareness. Premium gives interpretation.

### Free

- Stability Score
- Historical simulations
- Basic financial concepts
- Budgeting and goals
- Macro market context

### Premium

- Portfolio-linked insights
- Behavioral pattern analysis
- Advanced simulation
- Pattern-triggered mentorship
- Read-only investment-account behavioral analysis
- Market context connected to personal behavior

The key difference isn't feature quantity.

It is the depth of personalization.

**Free:**

> **How am I doing?**

**Premium:**

> **Why am I behaving this way, and what should I do differently?**

This is a proposed business model, not validated monetization.

---

# 16 — AI: Useful in the MVP, Not Forced Into the Redesign

AI was part of the hackathon MVP.

Its primary use was **generating finance quizzes**.

That supported the original education problem:

> **How can we generate learning content?**

When I later reframed RupeeRise around behavioral decision-making, I didn't force AI into the redesigned product simply because the hackathon required AI integration.

The new product problem was different:

> **How can the product influence decision behavior?**

I would explore AI further for personalized behavioral explanations or decision feedback, but that was not implemented or validated in this project.

So I would not claim AI-powered behavioral analysis or AI-driven risk scoring.

# 17 — What Actually Shipped

## Separating the hackathon MVP from the later redesign

During the hackathon, the developer and I produced a **functional MVP** with a rudimentary UI.

The implementation supported the broader finance experience and could generate quizzes using AI.

My designed UI screens were **not integrated** into that working implementation.

The later behavioral-finance system is therefore a **conceptual product redesign**, not a claim that the redesigned V2 was production-built.

# 18 — What I Would Measure

The redesigned product wasn't launched, so I don't have real behavioral or business outcomes.

Instead, I would validate the product thesis through leading indicators.

## Thesis Log completion — ~70% target

The Thesis Log is intentionally mandatory.

If completion is substantially lower, that would suggest the intervention is creating too much friction.

The metric tests whether users accept the pause.

## Simulation — 3+ sessions

One simulation could simply represent curiosity.

Repeated sessions would be a stronger indication that users are treating simulation as rehearsal.

The initial hypothesis:

> **3+ sessions before real-trade intent.**

## Stability Score — positive 30-day direction

A behavioral intervention shouldn't be judged from one session.

I'd look for directional change over approximately 30 days.

The question isn't:

> “Did the score increase?”

It is:

> **Are users showing more stable decision patterns over time?**

## Premium — ~20% initial hypothesis

The purpose of this metric is to test whether personalized behavioral interpretation creates enough value beyond the free awareness layer.

If conversion is weak, I'd revisit whether the premium boundary actually solves a meaningful user problem.

These are **proposed validation targets, not achieved results**.

---

# 19 — Limitations

## What I know — and what I don't

The research was small and exploratory.

Approximately 10 people were interviewed.

The findings overlap rather than form mutually exclusive groups.

### What I can say

- At least 6 participants recognized fragmented/incomplete financial information from finfluencer content.
- At least 4 described behavioral difficulties such as FOMO, overtrading or difficulty judging risk.
- Some participants were already investing or trading.
- The research was useful for challenging my original assumption.

### What I cannot claim

- That all young investors experience these problems.
- That the 4 behavioral participants were all active traders.
- That the behavioral pattern is statistically representative.
- That the Stability Score improves financial behavior.
- That users would pay for the premium model.
- That the redesign was validated.

There was no:

- V2 usability testing
- Live product validation
- Longitudinal behavioral data
- Validated conversion data

So the redesigned RupeeRise remains a **product hypothesis**.

# 20 — What I Would Do Differently

## I wouldn't start by designing the Stability Score.

If I ran RupeeRise again, I'd first validate whether users actually want to understand their own financial behavior.

I'd focus on three questions.

### Do users recognize the patterns?

A pattern that seems obvious to the product team may not feel meaningful to the user.

### Does reflection change the next decision?

Awareness alone isn't enough.

If a user sees:

> “You tend to react to rapid price movements.”

but makes the exact same decision next time, the intervention hasn't achieved its purpose.

### Where does useful friction become annoying friction?

Too little friction and RupeeRise becomes another fast-moving investment interface.

Too much friction and users abandon the experience.

That tension is the real design problem I would explore next.

> **The next version of RupeeRise isn't about adding more financial features. It's about finding the smallest intervention that can actually change what a user does at the moment a financial decision matters.**

# Reflection

RupeeRise changed how I think about the difference between **giving users information** and **helping users make decisions**.

I started with a familiar assumption:

> **People need more financial education.**

The research didn't make that assumption completely wrong.

It made it incomplete.

People were already consuming financial information. The more interesting problem was what happened when they had to apply that information under uncertainty, risk or emotion.

That changed the product direction from simply adding more financial content toward designing **moments of reflection, rehearsal and behavioral feedback**.

The most important thing I would carry forward from RupeeRise is not the Stability Score itself.

It's the question behind it:

> **When a user already knows enough, what can the product do at the exact moment knowledge needs to become a decision?**
