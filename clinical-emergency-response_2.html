THE PROBLEM

Scanning, deciding, and acting were competing for the same moment.

The hospital already had an emergency alert app, built by another vendor — fully functional, in daily use, missing nothing a clinical team needed. But an alert fired, and the same screen that told a clinician something was wrong also asked her to read patient details, find the right group chat, and decide who to call, all at once, often on a night shift.

On a floor where response time is a real number, that's the actual cost of the redesign — not a UI complaint.

I worked on this with the Product Manager, two clinical stakeholders, and engineering, through a stack change mid-project (React + Material → Ionic) and about 1–2 months of the workflow itself still being decided. Most of what follows was designed without a locked spec.

The Opportunity
How do we get from alert to coordinated action within 30 seconds — without making the interface harder to parse under pressure?
THE PRINCIPLE

Separate scanning from acting.

A clinician should understand what's happening before the interface asks her to do anything about it. That single rule drives every decision below.

Before
Alert, patient context, and available actions all surfaced on arrival — everything competing for attention in the same five seconds.
After
Alert → Scan → Act. Three distinct moments. Comprehension first, action one deliberate step later.
DECISION 01

Fast access loses to fast, correct understanding.

Before
The emergency screen tried to expose patient information, actions, communication, and deeper context together, all on arrival.
Decision
I moved the first interaction toward comprehension rather than immediate action.
After
The first screen prioritizes patient, location, emergency type, severity. Actions remain one step away.
Option A — not shipped
Fastest access to any single action — at the cost of asking the clinician to parse and decide simultaneously.
Option B — shipped
Slower to any single action. Faster to a correct one.
Why
The user needs enough context to make the next action correctly — not simply the fastest possible tap.
DECISION 02

Separate purpose, connected navigation.

Before
Alerts and communication lived in the same space, which is why they kept colliding on screen. Status showed only as a colored pill; actions sat behind an overflow menu, separate from the case itself.
Decision
Split the product into two purposes, then reconnected them through interaction instead of structure.
After
Patient → chat → patient details → chat — no re-searching for the same case twice.

Reachable from chat
The specific patient detail a clinician needs mid-conversation — MRN, DOB, contact, floor and cath lab location — sits one tap from the chat itself. She checks it and returns without losing the thread.
Before
Status as a colored pill only. EMS details and status changes sit behind an overflow menu.
After
Status as a full-width banner. Chat, patient details, and case actions on one card — with a dedicated Group tab for communication.
Also explored, not shipped
A third direction I explored alongside the two above: status still reads as a full-width banner, but adds a live ETA progress bar per case and trims card actions down to just view and message icons.

[Pending: what made this not the one that shipped — the ETA bar, the reduced actions, something else?]
Why
Separate spaces reduce cognitive noise. Connected navigation prevents the separation from creating extra work.

DECISION 03

The request sounded like typography. It was hierarchy.

Request
Stakeholders asked for bigger text — night-shift use, older staff, needs to be readable at a glance.
First attempt
Increased type size by ~2px. Didn't hold — the real problem was density, not scale.
Shipped
Larger cards, more spacing, stronger hierarchy, critical information given more visual weight. Cost some screen space — accepted, since staff usually handle one emergency at a time.
First attempt
Type size increased ~2px, density unchanged — didn't solve it
Shipped
Larger cards, reduced density, stronger hierarchy
CONSTRAINTS

Shipping a real improvement beat delaying for a complete overhaul.

Workflow ambiguity
The workflow moved for roughly 1–2 months while I kept designing without a locked spec.
Technology shift
React + Material became Ionic mid-build, forcing a rebuild of the component approach.

I didn't redesign everything. I left what already worked and focused effort on the surfaces that mattered under pressure.

VALIDATION

Tested with the people who use this every day.

2
Clinical stakeholders — nurse leadership, clinical practitioner
5
Nurses tested the redesigned experience directly

Feedback centered on readability, hierarchy, whether alert/communication separation held together, and how naturally patient context and messaging connected. Readability was the one that took more than a single pass to actually land.

OUTCOME

A more comfortable emergency experience.

  • 01 Clearer emergency scanning — patient, location, and severity readable at a glance.
  • 02 Separate communication without disconnected workflows.
  • 03 Patient context available throughout the response, not just at the start.
  • 04 Implemented and actively used by the hospital.

Clinical staff and leadership described it as more comfortable and intuitive than the dense, Epic-like systems they were used to.

30s
The 30-second target shaped the design; it was not a measured post-launch performance metric. If I were tracking it today: time from alert to first coordinated action, and readability-related support volume.
REFLECTION

This wasn't about removing functionality — the app already had what it needed. The redesign changed when information appeared, where it lived, and how the pieces stayed connected, instead of leaving a clinician to hold all of it in her head at 3am.
