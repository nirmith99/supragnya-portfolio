The Starting Point

I was handed an existing emergency response app used by hospital staff to manage STEMI cardiac alerts — one of the most time-critical events in healthcare. The app worked. It wasn't broken. But the moment I opened it, the problem was obvious: everything was stuffed into a single card. The alarm list and chat were combined in one view, presumably for convenience.

In practice, it meant nurses were expected to scan patient status, read messages, and decide on actions — all at once, all from the same cluttered surface. The app was also being integrated with Epic later on, which made structural clarity even more critical.

"If a 45-year-old nurse opens this app at 3 AM, she needs to understand the patient's situation and connect with a doctor in 30 to 40 seconds."

That sentence became the lens for every decision I made.

02

What Was Actually Wrong

The existing design wasn't visually broken — it was cognitively broken. At the moment a STEMI alarm fires, a nurse needs to do three things in sequence: understand what's happening, decide how to respond, then act. The old app asked her to do all three simultaneously from a single dense screen.

Alarm details, chat threads, action buttons, and patient information competed equally for attention. Nothing told the eye where to look first. There was no visual hierarchy communicating urgency. The most critical piece of information — time elapsed since the alarm — was buried among other data at the same visual weight.

The problem wasn't missing features. It was mental effort arriving at the worst possible moment.

03

The Constraint I Had to Design Around

Mid-project, the development stack shifted. The team was using Material Design and Ionic components in combination, and the expectation was that I would design within those constraints rather than introduce a new visual system. The problem was that Ionic components don't behave the same way as Material components — interactions, spacing, and elevation work differently — which meant designs that looked right in Figma sometimes fell apart in implementation.

Instead of pushing for a full visual overhaul, I focused my effort on the surfaces that directly affected emergency response behavior: the alarm card and the communication layer. That's where the real impact was.

04

The Decisions I Made

01
Separated alarm list from chat entirely

Alarms and chat lived in the same card. The reasoning was convenience — one place for everything. But in a cardiac emergency, mixing communication with triage creates noise at exactly the wrong moment. I restructured the experience into two distinct layers: the Alarm List for emergency alerts and patient status, and a Communication Hub for coordination. Separating these surfaces reduced visual noise without reducing any capability.

02
Made time elapsed the dominant element

In cardiac care, time is the metric everything else is measured against. Door-to-balloon time determines outcomes. Yet in the original design, time elapsed sat at the same visual weight as Case ID, provider contact, and facility name. I made the time elapsed counter the dominant element on each alarm card — large, high-contrast, impossible to miss. This wasn't a styling choice. It was an information hierarchy decision.

03
Status badge always visible — no tapping required

The previous design required opening a card to understand what stage a patient's case was in. I moved the status badge — showing Under Review, Accepted, or Pending — to always be visible on the alarm list card itself. A nurse managing multiple active alarms needs situational awareness across all of them before deciding where to focus. The badge gives her a complete picture of the floor at a glance.

04
Scaled cards up and reduced elements simultaneously

The doctor's feedback across every review was consistent: "The text needs to be bigger." Early iterations increased font size incrementally — it wasn't enough. The real fix wasn't typography alone, it was scale and density together. I increased overall card size, reduced the number of elements competing for space, and accepted that cards would expand vertically. Giving each card room to breathe meant critical information registered faster at 2 or 3 AM.

Time elapsed dominates the card. Status badge (Under Review / Accepted) is always visible without opening. Action icons are reduced and grouped. Each card gives complete situational context at a glance — no drilling required before the first decision.

The product shipped and went live in hospital workflows. The alert flow consistently met the sub-40-second response target the client had set. Staff described the experience as noticeably less stressful than parallel systems they were using.

The impact wasn't visual. It was behavioral. Nurses hesitated less before their first action. The system felt calmer — not because it looked different, but because it asked less of people at the moments that mattered most.

06

What I'd Do Differently

With more direct access to nurses, I would have run structured observation sessions during night shifts — not interviews, but watching how they actually hold the phone, what angle they read at, how they switch between this app and Epic mid-task. A lot of the density and scale decisions were made from secondhand, PM-filtered feedback. Seeing the context firsthand would have gotten us there faster.

I'd also push harder earlier on separating the design system from the Ionic constraint. The component mismatch cost real time in back-and-forth with engineering that could have been resolved in week one with a clearer handoff framework.
